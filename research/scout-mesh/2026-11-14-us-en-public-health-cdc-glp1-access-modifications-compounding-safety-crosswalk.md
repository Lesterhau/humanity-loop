## Direct primary-source observations

1. **CDC MMWR, 2026-11-12:** [Use of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Medications and Modified Medication Use Among Adults — United States, 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7544a1.htm) (75(44):645–651). CDC analyzed its July–August **2025** Rapid Surveys System survey (8,118 respondents; 976 self-reported GLP-1 users). Weighted **9.8%** of adults reported GLP-1 use in the preceding 12 months. Among users, **22.6%** reported a compounded product and **8.8% did not know** whether theirs was compounded. **44%** reported some modification from the prescribed use, including **28%** reporting skipped doses. **Among users who reported modifying use**, **38% cited cost** and **36% availability**. These last two percentages must NOT be represented as percentages of all adults or all GLP-1 users. Younger adults (18–39) were more likely than those 65+ to report modifications (54% versus 30%), but the survey cannot prove cause or outcomes.
2. **FDA current consumer guidance:** [FDA's Concerns with Unapproved GLP-1 Drugs Used for Weight Loss](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fdas-concerns-unapproved-glp-1-drugs-used-weight-loss). Compounded drugs are **not FDA-approved** and do not undergo FDA premarket review for safety, effectiveness or quality. FDA recognizes certain patient-specific medical circumstances for legitimate compounding; this is NOT a claim that every compounded product is illegal or that all compounded users are harmed. FDA flags dosing errors, mislabeled or fraudulent products, unsafe shipping temperature, salt-form differences, and misleading equivalence claims; it recommends using prescriptions from a clinician and state-licensed pharmacies and discussing medicine questions with a health professional.
3. **FDA regulatory context, 2026-02-06:** [FDA statement on non-approved GLP-1 marketing](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-intends-take-action-against-non-fda-approved-glp-1-drugs). This is enforcement-intent context, not proof of the legality of any particular patient's product or that a named vendor has been adjudicated liable.

## Evidence quality and limitations

- CDC's data describe **2025 self-reported behavior**, published November 12, 2026. They do not measure current November 2026 utilization, clinical harm, causal effect of cost, or efficacy of alternative dosing.
- CDC states panel cumulative response rates were **13.3%** and **3.8%** (two panels); weighting reduces but does not eliminate selection/nonresponse bias. Recall/measurement bias and lower internet access are additional limitations.
- The CDC estimates and FDA safety warnings address related but different questions. A survey respondent's reported medication modification is **not proof of misuse or injury**. FDA adverse-event reports do not by themselves establish causality or a comparative incidence rate.
- Avoid an unconditional claim that all compounded products have the same active ingredient as approved products: FDA notes different semaglutide salt forms and counterfeit/mislabeled products.

## Public-interest opportunity, bounded

**Practical service gap to investigate:** whether clinicians, pharmacies and benefit navigators routinely ask about medication affordability, fill delays, and confusion over whether a dispensed GLP-1 is compounded. A neutral, human-reviewed patient-information aid could encourage patients to verify their dispensing pharmacy, discuss dose/fill problems with their prescriber rather than improvise, and check FDA safety notices. This is a proposed educational aid, **not a verified access defect**, treatment plan, medical advice or evidence of beneficiary benefit.

**UNSENT draft for qualified clinical review:**
> A CDC survey of adults conducted in 2025 found that 44% of respondents who used GLP-1 medicines reported changing how they took them compared with their prescriptions. Among those who made changes, cost and availability were common reasons. If cost, delayed refills or uncertainty about a compounded product affects your prescription, discuss it with your prescribing clinician and pharmacist rather than independently changing doses. FDA-approved and compounded medicines are not regulated identically; FDA advises obtaining medicines through state-licensed pharmacies and checking with a health professional about questions or possible problems. This is general information, not individualized medical guidance.
