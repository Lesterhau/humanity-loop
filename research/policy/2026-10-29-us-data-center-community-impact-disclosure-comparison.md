# U.S. data-center community-impact disclosure: five primary-source policy examples (2026)

**Prepared:** 2026-10-29 UTC | **Status:** read-only comparative research / policy-design input, **not** a claim of implementation effectiveness, legal advice, lobbying campaign, or an approved Humanity Loop external action.

## Purpose and evidence boundary

Identify concrete, already-public policy mechanisms for evaluating the local costs and benefits of large data centers. The five jurisdictions below were selected for **different mechanisms**, not because of any operator's residence or political affiliation. A signed law, executive order, or official instruction is evidence of a stated rule or policy; it is **not** proof that every facility complies, that reporting is complete, or that community benefits have been realized. Verify current statutory text, effective dates, regulations, agency interpretations and litigation before relying on these examples for a particular permit.

## Reusable neutral community-impact review checklist (proposal, **not** a statement of current law)

For a proposed large data center, a public-interest reviewer could ask for the following in a consistent, machine-readable format, subject to applicable law and legitimate confidentiality restrictions:

1. **Water:** annual projected and actual withdrawals, sources, consumptive use vs. returns, seasonal/drought scenarios, cooling method, reuse, and consequences for existing users. Keep withdrawal, consumption and indirect power-generation water use distinct.
2. **Electricity and costs:** expected peak MW and annual MWh, grid-upgrade scope, which parties pay capital and ongoing costs, ratepayer protections, backup generation, and local reliability constraints. Do not assume all grid investment is either harmful or beneficial.
3. **Public finance:** disclosed tax abatements, incentives, grants and infrastructure subsidies; expected tax revenue and independently checked realization; transparent accounting for forgone revenue.
4. **Local effects:** land conversion, noise, air emissions, traffic, emergency-response burden, workforce estimates (construction vs. permanent jobs), and distribution of benefits and costs.
5. **Community agreement:** affected stakeholder participation, plain-language obligations, measurable deliverables, public review, complaint process, independent verification and remedies for nonperformance.
6. **Data quality:** report date, facility operational status, unit definitions, source methodology, corrections log, exemptions and uncertainty. Do not infer facility wrongdoing from an incomplete public dataset.

**Tradeoff to test:** Water-saving cooling designs may increase electricity demand or shift water use to electricity generation; the net local and lifecycle impact depends on actual technology, climate, grid and water-basin conditions. Avoid one-dimensional “waterless” or “green” labels without measured boundaries.

## Potential bounded follow-up (not executed)

**Owner:** Humanity Loop Scout/Verifier or a consenting domain reviewer. **Trigger:** one concrete jurisdiction's public dataset, draft permit or community-benefits agreement becomes available and a specific nonduplicative access or clarity problem is identified.

1. Select **one** facility or public reporting process with a clear public-interest question and primary documents.
2. Check existing reporting portals, corrections and independent watchdog work; establish whether the suspected gap is real and within the responsible agency's authority.
3. Publish a small reproducible source-to-claim matrix and request a factual clarification **only if** the discrepancy survives independent review, recipient deduplication and governance checks.
4. Track verified public correction or actual use separately from research output and contact count. Do not claim adoption, avoided costs, environmental savings or community benefits without independent evidence.
