## What the USDA source actually says

USDA's own November 10 public-inspection text describes a proposed regulatory codification of changes already made by **Public Law 119-21 (July 4, 2025)**, plus **additional discretionary proposals**. Therefore it would be inaccurate to tell recipients that all the underlying statutory changes are merely proposed or that the new NPRM alone already finalizes the discretionary changes.

1. The statute changed the SNAP three-month-in-36-month time-limit population to generally **ages 18–64** where no exception applies, and changed the dependent-child exception to responsibility for a child **under age 14**. Other exceptions can apply. USDA explicitly distinguishes the general work requirements (generally ages 16–59) from the time limit, so a person aged **60–64** may be outside general work registration but still subject to the time limit absent another exception. This is not a blanket declaration that every adult aged 60–64 must work or loses SNAP.
2. The statute changed geographic waiver criteria: in most states, unemployment **over 10%**; separate Alaska/Hawaii treatment at **at least 1.5 times** the national rate. USDA also describes new statutory exceptions for certain American Indian/Urban Indian/California Indian individuals and removal of prior categorical time-limit exceptions for homelessness, veteran status, and some former foster youth. Other exemption routes may still apply.
3. The NPRM separately proposes **stricter waiver evidence requirements** (generally a recent 12-month unemployment average from BLS/cooperating agencies, with limited exceptional-circumstance/limited-data alternatives), a narrower definition of geographic waiver area, and removing **“imminently homeless”** from SNAP's regulatory definition of homeless individual. The agency notes homelessness still affects other SNAP rules such as the shelter deduction. These discretionary provisions are **not final simply because the NPRM was published**.
4. USDA says the proposed rule is economically significant and discusses administrative costs, notices, screening and burden estimates. The numbers in its regulatory impact material are modeled administrative burden/transfer effects, **not verified individual benefit denials**.
